See What’s Inside the Ocean: Five Zones from Top to Bottom

The ocean is layered by light and pressure. Scroll down to descend through each zone, as scientists describe them.

0–200 m

Sunlight zone

Enough light reaches this top layer for photosynthesis, so nearly all marine plants and algae live here. It is warm, busy and home to most familiar sea life.

Life: Dolphins, tuna, sea turtles, coral reefs, phytoplankton

200–1,000 m

Twilight zone

Only a faint blue glimmer reaches here, and it fades fast. Many animals travel upward at night to feed, one of the largest daily migrations on Earth.

Life: Lanternfish, squid, hatchetfish, some sharks

1,000–4,000 m

Midnight zone

There is no sunlight at all, the water is cold and pressure is crushing. Many animals make their own light, called bioluminescence.

Life: Anglerfish, giant squid, sperm whales diving to feed

4,000–6,000 m

The abyss

Near-freezing water over wide plains of soft sediment. Food drifts down from above as “marine snow”, so life is sparse but specialised.

Life: Sea cucumbers, brittle stars, tripod fish

6,000–11,000 m

Hadal zone (trenches)

The deepest places on the planet, found in ocean trenches. Pressure is more than a thousand times that at the surface.

Life: Amphipods, snailfish, specialised microbes

See What Everyone Loves: Remarkable Ocean Animals

From the biggest animal alive to creatures that make their own light, these are the OceanSphere reader favourites.

Blue whale

The largest animal known to have lived, reaching around 30 metres. It feeds almost entirely on tiny shrimp-like krill, filtering huge mouthfuls of water.

Zone: sunlight, with deeper dives

Sea turtle

Ancient reptiles that cross whole oceans to feed and lay eggs on the beaches where they were born. Several species are threatened by nets, plastic and lost nesting sites.

Zone: sunlight, coasts

Manta ray

Gentle filter feeders with wingspans of several metres. They glide through plankton-rich water and visit cleaning stations on reefs.

Zone: sunlight, open water

Giant squid

A deep-sea mystery for centuries. It has some of the largest eyes in the animal kingdom, which help it detect the faint glow of moving animals.

Zone: twilight to midnight

Anglerfish

Lives in darkness and lures prey with a glowing lure grown from its own body, a stunning example of bioluminescence in action.

Zone: midnight

Clownfish

Small reef fish that live safely among stinging sea anemones. Each animal protects the other, a classic partnership known as mutualism.

Zone: sunlight, reefs

Explore. Choose. Enjoy. How the Ocean Moves

Choose a topic and enjoy the science behind the water that never stands still.

Surface currents

Wind pushes the top layer of the ocean into huge loops called gyres. Warm currents such as the Gulf Stream carry heat towards cooler regions and shape regional climate.

Deep circulation

Cold, salty water sinks and flows slowly along the seafloor, while warmer water moves near the surface. This global “conveyor belt” can take hundreds of years to complete a loop.

Tides

The Moon’s gravity pulls ocean water into bulges, giving most coasts two high and two low tides a day. Spring tides, the strongest, occur around new and full moon.

Waves

Most waves start with wind. The waves you see at the beach may have been created by a storm thousands of kilometres away.

Explore What’s Trending in Ocean Science

Look What’s New: four developments that are changing how we understand and care for the sea.

Marine heatwaves

Periods of unusually warm sea water can stress corals and kelp. Scientists track them using satellites and ocean buoys.

Ocean mapping

Only a small fraction of the seafloor has been mapped in fine detail. Sonar surveys and autonomous vehicles are filling in the blanks.

Seaweed and kelp farming

Growing seaweed needs no fresh water or fertiliser and is gaining interest for food and materials.

Blue carbon

Mangroves, seagrass and salt marshes store large amounts of carbon, and protecting them helps both climate and coasts.

Five Ways to Protect the Ocean

You do not need to live by the sea to help it. Most ocean problems begin on land, which means most solutions can too.

Why it matters

The ocean regulates climate, produces a large share of the oxygen we breathe, feeds billions of people and supports millions of jobs. Looking after it protects all of us.

  1. Cut single-use plastic

    A large share of marine litter starts on land. Carry a bottle and bag, and avoid items used for minutes but lasting for centuries.

  2. Choose sustainable seafood

    Look for local, in-season options and certified fisheries. Ask where and how your fish was caught.

  3. Save energy

    Burning fossil fuels warms and acidifies the ocean. Lower energy use at home is a direct way to help.

  4. Protect your beach

    Join a clean-up, stay off dunes and coral, and never take live shells or animals.

  5. Share what you learn

    Ocean literacy grows when friends and families talk about it. A short conversation can change habits.

Frequently asked questions

What is OceanSphere?

OceanSphere is an ocean science explainer. It covers ocean zones, currents, tides, marine life and conservation in plain language for curious readers and students.

How much of Earth is covered by ocean?

Oceans cover about 71 percent of Earth’s surface and hold most of the planet’s water.

How deep is the ocean?

The average depth is roughly 3,700 metres. The deepest known point, Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench, is nearly 11,000 metres down.

What causes tides?

Tides are caused mainly by the Moon’s gravity, with a smaller effect from the Sun. Their combined pull makes ocean water rise and fall.

Why are coral reefs important?

Reefs cover a tiny share of the seafloor but shelter a very large share of marine species. They also protect shorelines and support local fishing and tourism.

How can I help protect the ocean?

Reduce single-use plastic, choose sustainable seafood, save energy and join local beach clean-ups. Small habits add up when many people share them.
